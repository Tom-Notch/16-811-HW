      \item Consider a plane curve $y(x)$ over the interval $[x_0, x_1]$, with specified endpoints $y_0 = y(x_0)$ and $y_1 = y(x_1)$.
            Assume that $y_0 > 0$ and $y_1 > 0$ and that $y(x) \geq 0$ for $x_0 \leq x \leq x_1$.
            Now imagine rotating the curve about the x-axis to obtain a surface of revolution.
            Find the $C^2$ curve $y(x)$ with specified endpoints that minimizes the surface area of this surface of revolution.

                  [Hint: This problem explores further some of the limitations of the Calculus of Variations.
                        Depending on the endpoint conditions there may or may not be a $C^2$ solution.
                        What does the optimal “curve” look like when it is not $C^2$?
                        Can you say how the endpoint conditions matter?
                        Be aware: There are many subtleties; don't expect to cover all, but explore what you can.]

            \textbf{Solution:}

            Surface area of revolution is given by
            $$
                  S = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} 2\pi y(x) \sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2} dx.
            $$
            We want to minimize $S$ subject to the constraints $y(x_0) = y_0, y(x_1) = y_1$, so the optimization program is
            \begin{align*}
                  \min_y J(y) & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} F(x, y, y') dx, \\
                  s.t.        & \begin{cases}
                        y(x_0) & = y_0 \\
                        y(x_1) & = y_1
                  \end{cases}
            \end{align*}
            Where $F(x, y, y') = 2\pi y(x) \sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2}$.

            Now suppose $y^*$ minimizes $J$, and $\eta \in C^2 [x_0, x_1]$ be a random $\Delta$ function s.t. $\eta(x_0) = \eta(x_1) = 0$.
            And we define $y(x) = y*(x) + \epsilon \eta(x)$, where $\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}$ is a small number.
            Then we can define $J(S)$ w.r.t. $\epsilon$ as
            \begin{align*}
                  \Phi (\epsilon) & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} F(x, y, y') dx                                                     \\
                                  & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} F(x, y^*(x) + \epsilon \eta(x), {y^*}'(x) + \epsilon \eta'(x)) dx.
            \end{align*}
            And we can calculate $\Phi'(0)$ as
            \begin{align*}
                  \Phi'(0) & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} \eta (x) dx + \int_{x_0}^{x_1} \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \eta'(x) dx               \\
                           & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} \left[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right) \right] \eta(x) dx.
            \end{align*}
            Because $y^*$ minimizes $J$, we have $\Phi'(0) = 0$. Also because $\eta$ is arbitrary, so we must have the Euler-Lagrange equation
            $$
                  \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right) = 0.
            $$
            Because we have
            \begin{align*}
                  \begin{cases}
                        \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}  & = 2\pi \sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2}                    \\
                        \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} & = 2\pi y(x) \frac{y'(x)}{\sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2}}
                  \end{cases}
            \end{align*}
            Also because $F(x, y(x), y'(x))$ does not explicitly depend on $x$, we can use the \textbf{\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beltrami_identity}{Beltrami Identity}} to simplify the equation as
            \begin{align*}
                  F - y'(x) \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}                                                                                     & = C = \text{constant} \Rightarrow \\
                  2\pi y(x) \sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2} - 2\pi y'(x) \frac{y(x) y'(x)}{\sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2}} = 2\pi \frac{y(x)}{\sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2}} & = C \Rightarrow                   \\
                  y'(x)                                                                                                                        & = \pm \sqrt{(Cy)^2 - 1}
            \end{align*}
            Note that the constant $C$ is absorbing all the constants in the equation, so we can solve the equation by separating variables and integrating both sides as
            $$
                  \int \frac{dy}{\sqrt{(Cy)^2 - 1}} = \pm \int dx.
            $$
            Then we get
            $$
                  y(x) = C_1 \cosh\left(\frac{x - C_2}{C_1}\right)
            $$
            Where constants $C_1$ and $C_2$ depends on the boundary conditions $(x_0, y_0)$ and $(x_1, y_1)$.

            The shape visualized with \href{https://www.desmos.com/calculator}{Desmos} looks like a thin vase with big top and bottom, which is reasonable since we want the shape to shrink a little before it reaches another boundary, in order to minimize the surface area.

      \item Using Calculus of Variations, show that the shortest curve between two points on a sphere is an arc of a great circle.
                  [Hints: There are different ways to solve this problem.
                        Here is one approach: Use spherical $(u, v)$ coordinates, where $x = R \sin v \cos u, y = R \sin v \sin u, z = R \cos v,$ with $R$ the radius of the sphere.
                        (Do not worry about singularities in the representation.)
                        Rephrase 3D arclength $\sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2}$ as a function of $u, v, du, dv$.
                        View the curve as a function $v(u)$.
                        Observe that u does not appear directly in the resulting integrand for arclength, so replace the Euler-Lagrange equation with another, as in lecture.
                        You may find the following identity useful:
                        $$
                              \int \frac{a dw}{\sqrt{\sin^4 w - a^2 \sin^2 w}} = - \sin^{-1}(\frac{\cot w}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{a^2} - 1}}) + k,
                        $$
                        where a and k are appropriate constants.]

            \textbf{Solution:}

            The arclength of a curve on a sphere is given by
            $$
                  L = \int_{u_0}^{u_1} R \sqrt{[v'(u)]^2 + \sin^2v(u)} du.
            $$
            Now remove constant linear parameter $R$ and let $F(u, v(u), v'(u)) = \sqrt{[v'(u)]^2 + \sin^2v(u)}$. The optimization program is formally constructed as
            \begin{align*}
                  \min_v J(v) & = \int_{u_0}^{u_1} F(u, v(u), v'(u)) du, \\
                  s.t.        & \begin{cases}
                        v(u_0) & = v_0 \\
                        v(u_1) & = v_1
                  \end{cases}
            \end{align*}
            Omitting all the derivation we did in Question 1, the Euler-Lagrange equation is
            $$
                  \frac{\partial F}{\partial v} - \frac{d}{du} \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial v'} \right) = 0.
            $$
            And we have
            \begin{align*}
                  \begin{cases}
                        \frac{\partial F}{\partial v}  & = \frac{\sin v(u) \cos v(u)}{\sqrt{[v'(u)]^2 + \sin^2 v(u)}} \\
                        \frac{\partial F}{\partial v'} & = \frac{v'(u)}{\sqrt{[v'(u)]^2 + \sin^2 v(u)}}
                  \end{cases}
            \end{align*}
            Similar to Q1, because $F(u, v(u), v'(u))$ does not explicitly depends on $u$, we can apply the \textbf{\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beltrami_identity}{Beltrami Identity}} to simplify the Euler-Lagrange equation as
            \begin{align*}
                  F - v'(u) \frac{\partial F}{\partial v'}                                           & = C = \text{constant} \Rightarrow                   \\
                  \sqrt{[v'(u)]^2 + \sin^2v(u)} - v'(u) \frac{v'(u)}{\sqrt{[v'(u)]^2 + \sin^2 v(u)}} & = C \Rightarrow                                     \\
                  \sin^2 v(u)                                                                        & = C \sqrt{[v'(u)]^2 + \sin^2 v(u)} \Rightarrow      \\
                  [v'(u)]^2                                                                          & = \frac{\sin^4 v(u)}{C^2} - \sin^2 v(u) \Rightarrow \\
                  v'(u)                                                                              & = \pm \sqrt{\frac{\sin^4 v(u)}{C^2} - \sin^2 v(u)}
            \end{align*}
            Separate variables and integrate both sides, then we can get
            \begin{align*}
                  \int \frac{C^2 dv(u)}{\sqrt{\sin^4 v(u) - C^2 \sin^2 v(u)}} & = \pm \int du.
            \end{align*}
            Using the identity from hint, we have
            \begin{align*}
                  - \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{\cot v(u)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{C^2} - 1}} \right) & = \pm u + C' \Rightarrow                                  \\
                  \cot v(u)                                                             & = - \sin(\pm u + C') \sqrt{\frac{1}{C^2} - 1} \Rightarrow \\
                  \tan v(u)                                                             & = - \frac{\frac{C}{\sqrt{1 - C^2}}}{\sin (\pm u + C')}
            \end{align*}
            Absorbing the signs and rearranging the constants, we can get
            \begin{align*}
                  v(u) = \arctan \left( \frac{C_1}{\sin(u + C_2)} \right)
            \end{align*}
            Where $C_1, C_2$ are constants depending on the boundary conditions $(u_0, v_0)$ and $(u_1, v_1)$.

            The shape visualized with \href{https://www.desmos.com/calculator}{Desmos} looks like a periodic function with a period of $2\pi$, where $C_1$ controls amplitude $C_2$ controls phase.
            This is reasonable since $u$ is the longitude angle ranging $[0, 2\pi]$, and $v$ is the latitude angle ranging $[0, \pi]$, so the curve should be periodic with a period of $2\pi$ since it's a function of $u$.

      \item In the brachistochrone problem (“ski-slope” in lecture), suppose the right endpoint $(x_1, y_1)$ is not specified exactly, but is merely constrained to satisfy an equation of the form $g(x, y) = 0$.
            Show that the optimizing curve $y(x)$ is perpendicular to the iso-contour $g(x, y) = 0$ at $(x_1, y_1)$.

                  [Hints: (i) Use an equation from lecture rather than derive everything from scratch.
                        (ii) Observe that the curves are written differently, one explicitly as $y(x)$, the other implicitly as $g(x, y) = 0$.
                        Think about how to compare the slopes of the two curves despite these different representations.]

            \textbf{Solution:}

            Similar to what was taught in the lecture, let $s$ denote arclength and let $v = \frac{ds}{dt}$ denote instant linear velocity along the curve, then the time to reach $(x_1, y_1)$ is given by
            \begin{align*}
                  J(y) & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} \frac{ds}{v}                                                           \\
                       & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} \frac{\sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2}}{v} dx                                      \\
                       & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-2g}} \int_{x_0}^{x_1} \frac{\sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2}}{\sqrt{y_0 - y(x)}} dx
            \end{align*}
            So formally, the optimization program is
            \begin{align*}
                  \min_y J(y) & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} F(x, y(x), y'(x)) dx, \\
                  s.t.        & \begin{cases}
                        y(x_0)      & = y_0 \\
                        g(x_1, y_1) & = 0
                  \end{cases}
            \end{align*}
            Where $F(x, y(x), y'(x)) = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2}}{\sqrt{y_0 - y(x)}}$.

            Now suppose $y^*$ minimizes $J$, and $\eta \in C^2 [x_0, x_1]$ be a random $\Delta$ function s.t. $\eta(x_0) = 0$, we can define the variation $y(x) = y^*(x) + \epsilon \eta(x)$, where $\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}$ is a small number.
            Then we can define $J(y)$ w.r.t. $\epsilon$ as
            \begin{align*}
                  \Phi (\epsilon) & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1(\epsilon)} F(x, y, y') dx                                                     \\
                                  & = \int_{x_0}^{x_1(\epsilon)} F(x, y^*(x) + \epsilon \eta(x), {y^*}'(x) + \epsilon \eta'(x)) dx.
            \end{align*}
            And we can calculate $\Phi'(0)$ as
            \begin{align*}
                  \Phi'(0) & = \frac{dx_1}{d \epsilon} F \bigg|_{x_1(0)} + \int_{x_0}^{x_1(0)} \left[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\eta(x) - \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \eta'(x) \right] dx                                                                             \\
                           & = \left( \frac{dx_1}{d \epsilon} F + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \eta(x) \right) \bigg|_{x_1(0)} + \int_{x_0}^{x_1(0)} \left[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right) \right] \eta(x) dx
            \end{align*}
            Because the right endpoint is constrained to only satisfy $g(x_1, y_1) = 0$, we can define the constraint to be $g(x_1(\epsilon), y^*(x_1(\epsilon)) + \epsilon \eta(x_1(\epsilon))) = 0$ and make $g'(0) = 0$ at the right endpoint
            \begin{align*}
                  g'(0)                                                                    = \frac{d}{d\epsilon} g(x_1(\epsilon), y^*(x_1(\epsilon)) + \epsilon \eta(x_1(\epsilon))) \bigg|_{\epsilon = 0}  & = 0 \Rightarrow                                       \\
                  g_x \frac{dx_1}{d\epsilon} + g_y {y^*}' (x_1(\epsilon)) \frac{dx_1}{d\epsilon} + g_y \eta(x_1(\epsilon)) + \epsilon g_y \eta'(x_1(\epsilon)) \frac{dx_1}{d\epsilon} \bigg|_{\epsilon = 0} & = 0 \Rightarrow                                       \\
                  g_x \frac{dx_1}{d\epsilon} + g_y {y^*}' (x_1(0)) \frac{dx_1}{d\epsilon} + g_y \eta(x_1(0))                                                                                                & = 0 \Rightarrow                                       \\
                  \frac{dx_1}{d\epsilon}                                                                                                                                                                    & = - \frac{g_y \eta(x_1(0))}{g_x + g_y {y^*}'(x_1(0))}
            \end{align*}
            Substitute $\frac{dx_1}{d\epsilon}$ back to $\Phi'(0)$, we have
            \begin{align*}
                  \Phi'(0) & = \left( - \frac{g_y \eta(x_1(0))}{g_x + g_y (y^*)'(x_1(0))} F + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \eta(x) \right) \bigg|_{x_1(0)} + \int_{x_0}^{x_1(0)} \left[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right) \right] \eta(x) dx \\
                           & = \left( - \frac{g_y}{g_x + g_y {y^*}'(x_1(0))} F + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right) \eta(x_1(0)) + \int_{x_0}^{x_1(0)} \left[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right) \right] \eta(x) dx                         \\
            \end{align*}
            In order to make $\Phi'(0) = 0$ for arbitrary $\eta(x)$, we must have both
            \begin{align*}
                  \begin{cases}
                        - \frac{g_y}{g_x + g_y {y^*}'(x_1(0))} F + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}                  & = 0 \\
                        \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right) & = 0
                  \end{cases}
            \end{align*}
            Combine them together, we have
            \begin{align*}
                  \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right)                    = \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{g_y}{g_x + g_y {y^*}'(x_1(0))} F \right) & = 0 \Rightarrow \\
                  \frac{y'(x)}{\sqrt{(y_0 - y(x))(1 + (y'(x))^2)}} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{g_y}{g_x + g_y {y^*}'(x_1(0))} \frac{\sqrt{1 + (y'(x))^2}}{\sqrt{y_0 - y(x)}} \right)                                                 & = 0             \\
            \end{align*}
            omg this looks horrible, let's try another way.

            Again similar to Q1 and Q2, since $F(x, y(x), y'(x))$ does not directly depend on $x$, we can apply the \textbf{\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beltrami_identity}{Beltrami Identity}} to simplify the Euler-Lagrange equation as
            \begin{align*}
                  F - y'(x) \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}                       & = C = \text{constant} \Rightarrow \\
                  F - y'(x) \left(\frac{g_y}{g_x + g_y {y^*}'(x_1(0))} \right) F & = C \Rightarrow                   \\
            \end{align*}
            If we looked at the right endpoint $(x_1, y_1)$, we have
            \begin{align*}
                  \frac{g_x}{g_x + g_y y(x_1)} F \bigg|_{x = x_1}                                             & = C \Rightarrow \\
                  \frac{g_x}{g_x + g_y y(x_1)} \frac{\sqrt{1 + (y'(x_1))^2}}{\sqrt{y_0 - y(x_1)}}             & = C \Rightarrow \\
                  \frac{g_x^2 (1 + [y'(x_1)]^2)}{(g_x^2 + 2 g_x g_y y(x_1) + g_y^2 [y(x_1)]^2)(y_0 - y(x_1))} & = C^2
            \end{align*}
            omg this looks horrible again, let's try another way.

            Let's examine the endpoint condition again, we have $g(x_1, y_1) = 0$, so the variations $\delta x$, $\delta y$ are related by
            \begin{align*}
                  g_x \delta x_1 + g_y \delta y_1 = 0 \Rightarrow \delta x_1 = - \frac{g_y}{g_x} \delta y_1
            \end{align*}
            And from some ChatGPT conversations, we learned the transversality condition
            \begin{align*}
                  \left[ F - y' \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right] \delta x_1 + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \delta y_1 = 0
            \end{align*}
            Substituting with the above equation, we have
            \begin{align*}
                  \left[ F - y' \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right] \left( - \frac{g_y}{g_x} \delta y_1 \right) + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \delta y_1 = 0 \Rightarrow \\
                  \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} - \left[ F - y' \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right] \frac{g_y}{g_x} \right) \delta y_1 = 0
            \end{align*}
            Since $\delta y_1$ is arbitrary, we must have
            \begin{align*}
                  \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} - \left[ F - y' \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right] \frac{g_y}{g_x} = 0
            \end{align*}
            Substituting with $F - y' \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = C$, we have
            \begin{align*}
                  y' C - C(\frac{g_y}{g_x}) = 0 \Rightarrow y' = \frac{g_y}{g_x}
            \end{align*}
            Which exactly means that the optimizing curve $y(x)$ is perpendicular to the iso-contour $g(x, y) = 0$ at $(x_1, y_1)$.

      \item \begin{enumerate}
                  \item Using Lagrangian Dynamics, derive the relationship between joint torques and the angular state (angles, angular velocities, and angular accelerations) of the following balanced manipulator:
                        \begin{figure}[H]
                              \centering
                              \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figs/hw5_q4_fig1.png}
                        \end{figure}
                        There is no gravity (in practice, gravity is perpendicular to the sheet of the paper).

                        Legend: All of link \#1's mass, m1, is concentrated at distance $l_1$ from its rotational joint
                        (which is attached to the ground).
                        In turn, link \#2 rotates around this distal point, with two masses, $m_2$, located symmetrically, each at distance $l_2$, from the joint.
                        In practice, these two masses might constitute one counter-balanced end-effector or two different but equally weighted end-effectors.
                        — This is a variation of a basic SCARA-type robot arm, used in industrial assembly.

                        \textbf{Solution:}

                        The velocity of each mass is given by
                        \begin{align*}
                              \begin{cases}
                                    \dot{x}_1    & = - l_1 \sin \theta_1 \dot{\theta}_1                                                                                    \\
                                    \dot{y}_1    & = l_1 \cos \theta_1 \dot{\theta}_1                                                                                      \\
                                    \dot{x}_2    & = - (l_1 \sin \theta_1 + l_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)) \dot{\theta}_1 - l_2 \sin (\theta_1 + \theta_2) \dot{\theta}_2  \\
                                    \dot{y}_2    & = (l_1 \cos \theta_1 + l_2 \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)) \dot{\theta}_1 + l_2 \cos (\theta_1 + \theta_2) \dot{\theta}_2    \\
                                    \dot{x}_{2'} & = - (l_1 \sin \theta_1 - l_2 \sin (\theta_1 + \theta_2)) \dot{\theta}_1 + l_2 \sin (\theta_1 + \theta_2) \dot{\theta}_2 \\
                                    \dot{y}_{2'} & = (l_1 \cos \theta_1 - l_2 \cos (\theta_1 + \theta_2)) \dot{\theta}_1 - l_2 \cos (\theta_1 + \theta_2) \dot{\theta}_2
                              \end{cases}
                        \end{align*}

                        The Lagrangian of the system is given by
                        \begin{align*}
                              L  = & T - V                                                                                                                                                                                                                     \\
                              =    & T                                                                                                                                                                                                                         \\
                              =    & \frac{1}{2} m_1 (\dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{y}_1^2) + \frac{1}{2} m_2 (\dot{x}_2^2 + \dot{y}_2^2 + \dot{x}_{2'}^2 + \dot{y}_{2'}^2)                                                                                               \\
                              =    & \frac{1}{2} m_1 l_1 \dot{\theta_1}^2 + \frac{1}{2} m_2 \left[ (l_1^2 + l_2^2 + 2 l_1 l_2 \cos \theta_2) \dot{\theta_1}^2 + 2 l_2 (l_1 \cos \theta_2 + l_2) \dot{\theta_1}\dot{\theta_2}) + l_2^2 \dot{\theta_2}^2 \right] \\
                                   & + \left[ (l_1^2 + l_2^2 - 2 l_1 l_2 \cos \theta_2) \dot{\theta_1}^2 + 2 l_2 (- l_1 \cos \theta_2 + l_2) \dot{\theta_1}\dot{\theta_2}) + l_2^2 \dot{\theta_2}^2 \right]                                                    \\
                              =    & \left( \frac{1}{2} m_1 l_1^2 + m_2 (l_1^2 + l_2^2) \right) \dot{\theta_1}^2 + 2 m_2 l_2^2 \dot{\theta_1} \dot{\theta_2} + m_2 l_2^2 \dot{\theta_2}^2
                        \end{align*}
                        So the relationship between joint torques can be derived from the Euler-Lagrange equation as
                        \begin{align*}
                              \tau_1 & = \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}_1} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_1} \Rightarrow                \\
                              \tau_1 & = \frac{d}{dt} \left( \left( m_1 l_1^2 + 2 m_2 (l_1^2 + l_2^2) \right) \dot{\theta}_1 + 2 m_2 l_2^2 \dot{\theta}_2 \right) - 0 \Rightarrow \\
                              \tau_1 & = \left( m_1 l_1^2 + 2 m_2 (l_1^2 + l_2^2) \right) \ddot{\theta}_1 + 2 m_2 l_2^2 \ddot{\theta}_2
                        \end{align*}
                        And
                        \begin{align*}
                              \tau_2 & = \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}_2} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_2} \Rightarrow \\
                              \tau_2 & = \frac{d}{dt} \left( 2 m_2 l_2^2 \dot{\theta}_1 + 2 m_2 l_2^2 \dot{\theta}_2 \right) - 0 \Rightarrow                       \\
                              \tau_2 & = 2 m_2 l_2^2 \ddot{\theta}_1 + 2 m_2 l_2^2 \ddot{\theta}_2
                        \end{align*}
                  \item When $\ddot{\theta}_2 = 0$, explain the terms relating $\ddot{\theta}_1$ to $\tau_1$.

                        \textbf{Solution:}

                        When $\ddot{\theta}_2 = 0$, the relationship between $\ddot{\theta}_1$ and $\tau_1$ is given by
                        \begin{align*}
                              \tau_1 & = \left( m_1 l_1^2 + 2 m_2 (l_1^2 + l_2^2) \right) \ddot{\theta}_1
                        \end{align*}

                        In other words, the system is just a rigid body.
            \end{enumerate}
